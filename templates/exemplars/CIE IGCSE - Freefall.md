## Spec Point: Acceleration of free fall (Core)

### Defining Acceleration of Free Fall

**Acceleration of free fall** (symbol: $g$) is the acceleration of an object falling freely near the surface of the Earth.

**Key values:**
- $g \approx 9.8$ m/s² (near Earth's surface)
- $g$ is approximately constant for all objects near Earth's surface

> [!important] Critical Update - Use 9.8 m/s² from 2023 Onwards
> **November 2022**: Candidates must be made aware that from **2023 onwards**, all calculations involving $g$ (acceleration of free fall) must use the value **9.8 m/s²** (or 9.8 N/kg).
>
> **Previous practice:** Previously, the value 10 m/s² was often used for simplicity.
>
> **Current requirement:** Using 10 now may result in lost marks for accuracy.
>
> **Always use: $g = 9.8$ m/s²**

> [!tip] Understanding Acceleration of Free Fall (Teaching Point)
> **What is free fall?**
> Free fall is when an object falls under the influence of gravity alone, with no other forces acting (or with air resistance negligible).
>
> **Key concepts:**
> - **Near Earth's surface:** The value 9.8 m/s² applies when the object is close to Earth
> - **Approximately constant:** The value doesn't change significantly with height for everyday situations (buildings, aircraft)
> - **Independent of mass:** ALL objects fall with the same acceleration (when air resistance is negligible) - see misconceptions below
>
> **What $g = 9.8$ m/s² means:**
> - For every second of falling, the speed increases by 9.8 m/s
> - After 1 s: speed = 9.8 m/s
> - After 2 s: speed = 19.6 m/s
> - After 3 s: speed = 29.4 m/s

### $g$ is Constant During Free Fall

> [!important] Acceleration Does Not Change as Object Falls
> **May 2017**: Relatively few candidates recognized that the acceleration of free fall **does not change** as an object falls (in a vacuum).
>
> **Common misconception:**
> - ❌ Many incorrectly thought acceleration halved when the distance halved
> - ❌ Many thought acceleration increased as the object fell
>
> **Correct understanding:**
> - ✅ $g$ remains constant at 9.8 m/s² throughout the fall
> - ✅ The acceleration does NOT depend on how far the object has fallen
> - ✅ The acceleration does NOT depend on how long it has been falling
> - ✅ The acceleration does NOT depend on the current speed

**Why $g$ is constant:**
- Near Earth's surface, the gravitational field strength is approximately uniform
- The force (weight) acting on the object remains constant
- Since $F = ma$ and force is constant (for constant mass), acceleration is constant

> [!warning] Graph Interpretation Error - Acceleration Increasing
> **November 2016**: Even able candidates tended to opt for graphs showing that acceleration **increased** uniformly with time, rather than remaining constant.
>
> **The confusion:**
> - Students confuse increasing **speed** with increasing **acceleration**
> - They see speed going up and assume acceleration must also be going up
>
> **Correct interpretation:**
> - On a **speed-time graph** for free fall: Straight diagonal line (constant gradient)
> - Gradient = acceleration = constant = 9.8 m/s²
> - Speed increases, but at a **constant rate** (constant acceleration)

> [!tip] Free Fall on Speed-Time Graphs (Teaching Point)
> **To answer questions about free fall graphs, you must recall that acceleration due to free fall is constant.**
>
> **What this means for graphs:**
> - The **gradient** of a speed-time graph = acceleration
> - For free fall, gradient is constant
> - Therefore, the line must be **straight** (not curved)
>
> **Graph characteristics:**
> - **Straight diagonal line** with positive gradient
> - Gradient = 9.8 m/s² (or close to 10 m/s²)
> - Speed increasing at constant rate
> - It doesn't matter what direction the object is going in - this is a speed-time graph that doesn't rely on direction

### All Objects Fall with the Same Acceleration

> [!warning] Major Misconception - Heavier Objects Fall Faster
> **May 2018**: A major misconception is that heavier objects fall faster. The majority of candidates did not understand that (when air resistance is negligible), **all objects fall with the same acceleration** regardless of their mass.
>
> **Common incorrect beliefs:**
> - ❌ "Heavy objects fall faster than light objects"
> - ❌ "A 10 kg mass falls faster than a 1 kg mass"
> - ❌ "Larger mass means larger acceleration"
>
> **Correct understanding:**
> - ✅ ALL objects fall with acceleration $g = 9.8$ m/s² (when air resistance negligible)
> - ✅ A feather and a hammer fall at the same rate in a vacuum
> - ✅ Mass does NOT affect the acceleration during free fall

> [!tip] Why All Objects Fall at the Same Rate (Teaching Point)
> **May 2022**: Few candidates realized that because Weight ($W = mg$) is the driving force and $F = ma$, it follows that $mg = ma$, and therefore $a = g$. This explains why acceleration is independent of mass.
>
> **The physics explanation:**
>
> **Step 1: Weight is the driving force**
> - Weight = $mg$ (mass × gravitational field strength)
> - This is the force pulling the object down
>
> **Step 2: Newton's Second Law**
> - $F = ma$ (force = mass × acceleration)
> - For falling object: $F = W = mg$
>
> **Step 3: Combine the equations**
> - $F = ma$
> - But $F = mg$ (weight)
> - Therefore: $mg = ma$
>
> **Step 4: Cancel mass**
> - $mg = ma$
> - Divide both sides by $m$: $g = a$
> - **Therefore: acceleration = $g$ (independent of mass!)**
>
> **What this means:**
> - The mass cancels out in the equation
> - A heavier object has more weight, but also more inertia (harder to accelerate)
> - These two effects exactly cancel out
> - Result: All objects accelerate at the same rate in free fall

**Example demonstrating equal acceleration:**

Two objects dropped from the same height:
- Object A: mass = 1 kg, weight = 9.8 N, acceleration = 9.8 N ÷ 1 kg = 9.8 m/s²
- Object B: mass = 10 kg, weight = 98 N, acceleration = 98 N ÷ 10 kg = 9.8 m/s²
- Both reach the ground at the same time!

### Recognizing Free Fall from Graphs

> [!tip] Identifying Free Fall from Speed-Time Graphs (Teaching Point)
> **How to recognize free fall:**
>
> **Calculate the gradient:**
> - Use a triangle covering the whole diagonal line
> - Calculate: gradient = rise ÷ run = Δv ÷ Δt
>
> **Check if gradient ≈ 9.8 m/s²:**
> - If the value you calculated for gradient is similar or identical to 9.8 m/s², this indicates free fall
> - This means the object is falling under the force of its own weight only
>
> **Example:**
> - Speed increases from 0 to 49 m/s over 5 s
> - Gradient = 49 ÷ 5 = 9.8 m/s²
> - ✅ This is free fall!
>
> **Important:** It is important to notice that the line goes up to 49 m/s, not 50 m/s - differences like this are crucial when reading data from a graph.

**What free fall tells you:**
- No supporting force (e.g., cable broken, object dropped)
- Only weight acting (air resistance negligible)
- Acceleration = $g$ = 9.8 m/s²

### Calculating Weight Using $g$

**Weight** is the force due to gravity acting on a mass.

**Formula:**
$$W = mg$$

Where:
- $W$ = weight (N)
- $m$ = mass (kg)
- $g$ = acceleration of free fall = 9.8 m/s² (or 9.8 N/kg)

> [!warning] Common Error - Not Using $g$ in Calculations
> **May 2017**: When calculating the force required to lift a mass (related to weight), candidates often failed to multiply the mass by $g$.
>
> **The error:**
> - Given: mass = 5 kg
> - ❌ WRONG: Weight = 5 N (just using the mass value)
> - ✅ CORRECT: Weight = 5 × 9.8 = 49 N
>
> **Remember:** Weight is a FORCE (measured in newtons), not the same as mass!

**Worked Example:**

Calculate the weight of a 2.0 kg object near Earth's surface.

**Solution:**
- Formula: $W = mg$
- Substitution: $W = 2.0 \times 9.8$
- Calculation: $W = 19.6$ N

**Answer:** Weight = 19.6 N (or 20 N to 2 sig figs)

---

### Significant Figures and $g$

> [!tip] Rounding Answers with $g$ (Teaching Point)
> **Since $g = 9.8$ m/s² is given to 2 significant figures, answers involving free fall should generally be given to 2 significant figures.**
>
> **Examples:**
> - Weight of 2.0 kg = 2.0 × 9.8 = 19.6 N
>   - Can write as **19.6 N** (3 sig figs - acceptable)
>   - Or **20 N** (2 sig figs - also acceptable)
>
> - Acceleration calculated from graph = 9.76 m/s²
>   - Should write as **9.8 m/s²** (2 sig figs)
>   - Or **10 m/s²** (1 sig fig - less precise but shows it's close to $g$)
>
> **Key point:** Don't over-specify precision - match the precision of the given data.

## Spec Point: Motion of falling objects (Extended)

### Falling Without Air Resistance (In a Vacuum)

**When air resistance is negligible (e.g., in a vacuum or for dense, compact objects):**

**Motion characteristics:**
1. **Acceleration = $g$ = 9.8 m/s²** (constant throughout fall)
2. **Speed increases uniformly** (at constant rate)
3. **Only force acting = Weight** (downward)
4. **Resultant force = Weight** (constant)

**Speed-time graph:**
- Straight diagonal line (constant gradient)
- Gradient = 9.8 m/s²
- No terminal velocity reached

> [!tip] Why Force Causes Constant Acceleration (Teaching Point)
> **Physics connection:** A force (weight) is acting downwards on the object, causing it to accelerate in that direction.
>
> **Example - water drops falling:**
> - Weight acts downward on each drop
> - This causes drops to accelerate downward
> - **Result:** Drops closer to the ground are travelling at a greater speed than those higher up
> - They are increasing the distance from drops above
>
> **This explains:**
> - Why falling objects speed up
> - Why the gap between successive drops increases
> - Why speed increases uniformly when only weight acts

---

### Falling With Air Resistance (In Air or Liquid)

**When air resistance (drag) is present:**

The motion is more complex and occurs in stages.

#### **Stage 1: Initial Acceleration (Beginning of Fall)**

**At the moment of release:**
- Object starts from rest (speed = 0)
- Air resistance = 0 (because speed = 0)
- **Only force = Weight** (downward)
- **Resultant force = Weight** (maximum)
- **Acceleration = $g$ = 9.8 m/s²** (maximum)

#### **Stage 2: Decreasing Acceleration (Middle of Fall)**

**As the object speeds up:**
- Speed increases
- **Air resistance increases** (because it depends on speed)
- Resultant force = Weight - Air resistance (decreasing)
- **Acceleration decreases** (but still positive - still speeding up!)

> [!important] Air Resistance Increases with Speed
> **November 2022**: Few candidates correctly stated that **air resistance increases as velocity increases**. Many incorrectly linked air resistance to time.
>
> **Correct relationship:**
> - ✅ Air resistance increases as **speed** increases
> - ✅ Faster motion → more air resistance
>
> **Incorrect ideas:**
> - ❌ Air resistance increases with time (NO - it's speed, not time!)
> - ❌ Air resistance is constant (NO - it changes with speed!)
>
> **Key point:** Air resistance is a speed-dependent force.

> [!warning] Contradictory Statements About Acceleration
> **November 2022**: Weaker answers included contradictions, such as stating the object was **decelerating** while also saying its **speed was increasing**.
>
> **Clarification - This is the tricky part:**
> - ✅ Correct: "Acceleration decreases, but speed increases"
> - ✅ Correct: "Object is still speeding up, but not as quickly"
> - ❌ Incorrect: "Object is decelerating" (implies slowing down)
>
> **Key distinction:**
> - **Decreasing acceleration** = speeding up more slowly (acceleration getting smaller, but still positive)
> - **Deceleration** = slowing down (acceleration is negative)
>
> **Correct physics:** Until terminal velocity, the object is always speeding up (acceleration is positive), but the rate of speeding up decreases (acceleration decreases).

**The logic chain:**
1. Speed increases
2. → Air resistance increases (depends on speed)
3. → Resultant force decreases (Weight - larger air resistance)
4. → Acceleration decreases (from $F = ma$, smaller force = smaller acceleration)
5. But speed still increasing (just not as quickly)

#### **Stage 3: Terminal Velocity (End of Fall)**

**Eventually:**
- Speed increases to a point where Air resistance = Weight
- **Forces are balanced** (Weight down = Air resistance up)
- **Resultant force = 0**
- **Acceleration = 0**
- **Speed = constant** (terminal velocity reached)

**Terminal velocity** is the maximum constant speed reached by a falling object when forces are balanced.

> [!warning] Common Misconception - Speed Continues Increasing
> **May 2023**: More candidates thought that the speed of a falling object continued to increase **after** it reached terminal velocity, showing a lack of understanding of the zero resultant force condition.
>
> **Correct understanding:**
> - ✅ At terminal velocity: Resultant force = 0
> - ✅ If resultant force = 0, then acceleration = 0 (from $F = ma$)
> - ✅ If acceleration = 0, speed is **constant** (not increasing!)
>
> **Therefore:**
> - Speed does NOT continue increasing after terminal velocity
> - Object falls at constant speed from that point on

**Speed-time graph:**
- Starts as straight line (initial acceleration = $g$)
- Curves (gradient decreasing - acceleration decreasing)
- Flattens to horizontal line (terminal velocity - constant speed)

### Summary Table: Falling With and Without Air Resistance

| Feature | Without Air Resistance (Vacuum) | With Air Resistance (Air) |
|:--------|:-------------------------------|:--------------------------|
| **Forces acting** | Weight only | Weight and Air resistance |
| **Resultant force** | Constant (= Weight) | Decreases (Weight - Air resistance) |
| **Acceleration** | Constant (= $g$ = 9.8 m/s²) | Decreases from $g$ to 0 |
| **Speed** | Increases uniformly | Increases, then becomes constant (terminal velocity) |
| **Speed-time graph** | Straight diagonal line | Curved line flattening to horizontal |
| **Terminal velocity?** | No | Yes |

### Describing Motion of Falling Objects - Mark Scheme Requirements

**Common question:** "Describe and explain the motion of a falling object from release until it hits the ground." (3-5 marks)

> [!important] Terminology - Use "Weight" not "Gravity"
> **November 2022**: When describing falling motion with air resistance, references to "gravity" were insufficient; candidates must specify **"weight"** or **"force of gravity"**.
>
> **Acceptable terms:**
> - ✅ "Weight"
> - ✅ "Force of gravity"
> - ✅ "Gravitational force"
>
> **Too vague:**
> - ❌ "Gravity" (this is a field, not a force)
>
> **Why it matters:** Examiners want you to show you understand it's a **force** acting on the object.

**Required answer structure (for falling with air resistance):**

1. **Initial stage:**
   - "At release, speed = 0, so air resistance = 0"
   - "Weight acts downward" (or "force of gravity acts downward")
   - "Resultant force = Weight" (maximum)
   - "Acceleration = $g$ = 9.8 m/s²" (maximum)

2. **Middle stage:**
   - "As speed increases, air resistance increases"
   - "Resultant force = Weight - Air resistance" (decreasing)
   - "Acceleration decreases"
   - "But speed continues to increase" (just more slowly)

3. **Terminal velocity:**
   - "Eventually, Air resistance = Weight"
   - "Forces are balanced" (or "resultant force = 0")
   - "Acceleration = 0"
   - "Speed becomes constant" (terminal velocity)

**Typical mark allocation:**
- 1 mark: Initial acceleration = $g$ (or weight > air resistance)
- 1 mark: Air resistance increases as speed increases
- 1 mark: Resultant force / acceleration decreases
- 1 mark: Forces balance at terminal velocity
- 1 mark: Constant speed when forces balanced

### Confusion Between Speed and Acceleration

> [!warning] Speed vs Acceleration - Common Error
> **May 2016**: There was widespread confusion where candidates believed that for a falling object, the **acceleration** increases from zero, rather than the **speed** increasing from zero.
>
> **The confusion:**
> - Students mix up which quantity starts at zero
> - They confuse "increasing speed" with "increasing acceleration"
>
> **Correct understanding:**
> - ✅ At start: **Speed = 0** (object at rest before release)
> - ✅ At start: **Acceleration = $g$ = 9.8 m/s²** (maximum, not zero!)
> - ✅ As object falls: **Speed increases** (from 0 upward)
> - ✅ With air resistance: **Acceleration decreases** (from $g$ toward 0)

**Example timeline:**

| Time | Speed | Acceleration (with air resistance) |
|:-----|:------|:----------------------------------|
| At release | 0 m/s | 9.8 m/s² (maximum) |
| After 1 s | 8 m/s (example) | 7 m/s² (decreasing) |
| After 2 s | 14 m/s | 4 m/s² (decreasing) |
| At terminal velocity | 20 m/s (constant) | 0 m/s² |

**Key point:** Speed increases FROM zero; acceleration decreases TO zero.

### Speed and Distance During Fall

> [!tip] Greatest Speed at Bottom (Teaching Point)
> **May 2019**: Most candidates recognized a falling ball has greatest speed at the bottom, but few related this to covering the lowest section in the shortest time.
>
> **The connection:**
> - Object is fastest at the bottom (highest speed)
> - Because it's moving fastest, it covers the last section of distance in less time
> - If you drop two balls 1 second apart, the gap between them increases as they fall
>
> **Why this matters:**
> - Speed = distance / time
> - Same distance, less time → higher speed
> - Explains why time intervals get shorter for equal distances when falling

### Calculating Forces and Acceleration During Fall

**Mathematical skills required:**

**1. Calculating resultant force:**
$$F_{\text{resultant}} = \text{Weight} - \text{Air resistance}$$

**2. Calculating acceleration:**
$$a = \frac{F_{\text{resultant}}}{m}$$

Or combined:
$$a = \frac{W - R}{m} = \frac{mg - R}{m} = g - \frac{R}{m}$$

Where:
- $W$ = weight (N)
- $R$ = air resistance (N)
- $m$ = mass (kg)
- $g$ = 9.8 m/s²

**Worked Example:**

A skydiver of mass 80 kg is falling. At a certain instant, air resistance = 600 N.

**Calculate the acceleration at this instant.**

**Solution:**

**Step 1:** Calculate weight
- $W = mg = 80 \times 9.8 = 784$ N

**Step 2:** Calculate resultant force
- $F_{\text{resultant}} = W - R = 784 - 600 = 184$ N

**Step 3:** Calculate acceleration
- $a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{184}{80} = 2.3$ m/s²

**Answer:** Acceleration = 2.3 m/s²

> [!important] Show Formula Before Substituting (Teaching Point)
> **Exam advice:** You can gain the mark for writing out the equation or substituting the numbers correctly into the equation.
>
> **Therefore:**
> - If you accidentally put in wrong numbers, you can still get a mark for providing the correct equation
> - This shows how important it is to show all of your working out in a clear and logical way
>
> **Good structure:**
> 1. State formula: $a = F / m$
> 2. Show substitution: $a = 184 / 80$
> 3. Calculate: $a = 2.3$ m/s²

### Graphing Falling Motion with Air Resistance

**Speed-time graph characteristics:**

**Shape:** Curve that starts steep, then flattens to horizontal

**Drawing the graph:**
1. Starts at origin (0, 0) if dropped from rest
2. Initially steep gradient (≈ 9.8 m/s²)
3. Gradient decreases (curve flattening)
4. Ends as horizontal line (terminal velocity)

> [!tip] Drawing Tangents to Show Changing Acceleration (Teaching Point)
> **Teaching advice:** Draw tangents to the graph at several points, so the gradient change is clear.
>
> **Method:**
> 1. Draw tangent at early time (e.g., t = 1 s) - steep gradient
> 2. Draw tangent at middle time (e.g., t = 5 s) - less steep gradient
> 3. Draw tangent at late time (terminal velocity section) - zero gradient
>
> **This shows:**
> - Gradient (acceleration) starts high
> - Gradient decreases over time
> - Gradient becomes zero at terminal velocity
>
> **Drawing as much as you can on the graph makes it easier to calculate the gradient.**

> [!tip] Using Guide Lines for Graph Sketching (Teaching Point)
> **Secret to good graph drawing:**
>
> Teachers, textbooks and revision sites use guides which they rub out before showing their work. In an exam you should do this too.
>
> **Technique:**
> 1. Mark key points with small crosses
> 2. Draw light guide lines to help shape the curve
> 3. Draw smooth curve through/along guides
>
> **Another secret:** You can leave them all in place and the examiner won't mind a bit. They will just mark your slope.
>
> **For falling with air resistance:**
> - Mark starting point (0, 0)
> - Mark where you estimate terminal velocity starts (horizontal guide line)
> - Draw smooth curve connecting them, getting less steep

### Calculating Acceleration from Curved Graphs (Tangents)

**At a specific time on a curved speed-time graph:**

**Method:**
1. Draw a tangent to the curve at that time
2. Calculate the gradient of the tangent
3. Gradient = acceleration at that instant

**Example from June 2024 Paper 41:**
- "Tangent drawn at t = 0.010 s"
- "Acceleration = gradient of tangent"
- Calculate: gradient = Δv / Δt using two points on the tangent

**Step-by-step:**
1. Place ruler at the point on curve
2. Draw tangent (straight line just touching curve at that point)
3. Make tangent long enough to read two clear points
4. Read two points on tangent: (t₁, v₁) and (t₂, v₂)
5. Calculate: a = (v₂ - v₁) / (t₂ - t₁)

- Calculate resultant force and acceleration (2-3 marks)

**Mark Scheme Structure:**
- Often broken into parts: (a) describe motion, (b) calculate forces, (c) draw tangent, (d) determine terminal velocity
- "Describe" questions worth 3-5 marks
- Calculation questions worth 2-3 marks each
- Total question often 8-10 marks

---

#### Command Words for Motion of Falling Objects

| Command Word | What to Do | Common Traps |
|:------------|:-----------|:-------------|
| **"Describe and explain"** | Give sequence: initial acceleration → air resistance increases → forces balance → terminal velocity | ❌ Saying "gravity" instead of "weight"; contradicting yourself (deceleration vs decreasing acceleration) |
| **"Determine"** | Read terminal velocity from horizontal section of graph | ❌ Reading from wrong part of graph |
| **"Calculate"** | Find resultant force (W - R), then acceleration (F/m) | ❌ Not subtracting air resistance; not showing working |
| **"Draw"** | Sketch curve starting steep, flattening to horizontal | ❌ Drawing straight line; not flattening to horizontal |

#### Exam Strategy for Motion of Falling Objects

> [!tip] Maximizing Marks
> **For "Describe and explain" questions (4-5 marks):**
>
> **Follow this sequence (The Logic Chain):**
> 1. ✅ "Initially, speed = 0, air resistance = 0"
> 2. ✅ "Weight acts downward" (NOT just "gravity"!)
> 3. ✅ "Resultant force = Weight, acceleration = $g$ = 9.8 m/s²"
> 4. ✅ "As speed increases, air resistance increases"
> 5. ✅ "Resultant force decreases (Weight - Air resistance)"
> 6. ✅ "Acceleration decreases" (but speed still increasing!)
> 7. ✅ "When Air resistance = Weight, forces balanced"
> 8. ✅ "Resultant force = 0, acceleration = 0"
> 9. ✅ "Speed constant" (terminal velocity reached)
>
> **Key terms to use:**
> - ✅ "Weight" or "force of gravity" (NOT just "gravity")
> - ✅ "Air resistance increases with speed"
> - ✅ "Resultant force" or "unbalanced force"
> - ✅ "Forces balanced" (at terminal velocity)
> - ✅ "Constant speed" (at terminal velocity)
>
> **Avoid contradictions:**
> - ❌ DON'T say "decelerating" when you mean "acceleration decreasing"
> - ✅ DO say "acceleration decreases, but speed still increases"
>
> **For calculations (2-3 marks):**
> 1. ✅ State formula first: $F_{\text{resultant}} = W - R$
> 2. ✅ Calculate weight if not given: $W = mg$
> 3. ✅ Show substitution: $F = 784 - 600 = 184$ N
> 4. ✅ Use $F = ma$ to find acceleration: $a = F/m$
> 5. ✅ Include units (m/s² for acceleration, N for force)
>
> **For drawing tangents:**
> - ✅ Use a ruler (always!)
> - ✅ Draw tangent at the specified time
> - ✅ Make it long enough to read two clear points
> - ✅ Calculate gradient using points ON THE TANGENT (not on curve!)
> - ✅ Show working: state points, calculate Δv and Δt, divide
>
> **For sketching graphs:**
> - ✅ Start at origin (if dropped from rest) or given starting point
> - ✅ Initially steep (gradient ≈ 9.8)
> - ✅ Curve gradually flattening
> - ✅ End as horizontal line (terminal velocity)
> - ✅ You can use guide lines and leave them in!
>
> **Common errors to avoid:**
> - ❌ Saying "gravity" instead of "weight"
> - ❌ Linking air resistance to time (it depends on SPEED!)
> - ❌ Contradictions (deceleration while speeding up)
> - ❌ Thinking speed continues increasing after terminal velocity
> - ❌ Confusing speed starting at zero with acceleration starting at zero
> - ❌ Drawing straight line instead of curve
> - ❌ Not showing formula before calculation
>
> **Remember the logic chain:**
> Speed ↑ → Air resistance ↑ → Resultant force ↓ → Acceleration ↓ (but speed still ↑!) → Eventually forces balance → Terminal velocity (constant speed)
